Mark Von Ulrich

Died February 19, 2018 · New York City · Aged 65

Research updated October 13, 2026. Public records and personal recollections are attributed individually.

Life and career

  • He died in New York City on February 19, 2018, aged 65. The notice appeared on April 8; that is the publication date, not his death date. [obituary]
  • Originally from Toledo, Ohio, he studied at Columbus College of Art and Design, taught drawing at Ohio State University, and moved to New York in 1983. [obituary]
  • His LinkedIn profile lists college attendance in 1971–1975. [linkedin]
  • He worked at Tony Russell Design, founded Designthing, and served as creative director at Campbell Lewis. [obituary]
  • His illustration clients included MacWorld, Wired, Playboy, The New York Times, and Harvard Business Review. [obituary]
  • He collected contemporary and outsider art, including William Hawkins, Martín Ramírez, Ken Grimes, and Johann Hauser. His obituary connects his illustrations to circus posters and folk art. [obituary]
  • Friends knew him as “thing.” He served on the board of St. Andrew’s Golf Club. [obituary]
  • A 2017 United Hospital Fund report credits design to Mark Von Ulrich and Maria Kerdel-De Marco. [uhf]

Pamela and their collections

  • Pamela, his wife, met him through styling work. Her obituary says they developed Designthing together and supported emerging, contemporary, and outsider artists. [pamela]
  • They also collected found and made objects, notably objects depicting hands. The obituary identifies the hand collection as an early interest of Pamela’s. [pamela]

His own account of the digital work

  • In his 2005 email to Robin, he recalled attending art school with Alex Grey, whom he remembered as Alex Velsy. [email]
  • He dated his introduction to the Macintosh to 1984, through his friend Nilo Rodis, then described buying a Mac and illustrating for MacWorld alongside John Hersey and Scott Baldwin. [email]
  • He described drawing in an old SuperPaint version, painting in Photoshop, and deliberately preserving pixelation even as publishers favored smoother vector artwork. [email]

Connections for the symbolism study

The hand collection is a useful biographical connection to the recurring hands in the reference images. It does not establish that a particular illustration was inspired by a collected object.

Circus posters, folk art, and outsider-art collecting provide documented context for studying theatrical figures and dense patterning. Any link to a specific symbol remains an interpretation.

An exact birth date, cause of death, and artist-authored explanations of the individual symbols were not established by these sources.
