Mark Von Ulrich
A guide to symbolic direction

Give the idea a body, then make that body’s structure express the argument.

Use this guide to ask “What would Mark add?” when developing an illustration or scene. The useful inheritance is a way of constructing visual metaphors: an abstract activity becomes anatomy, an object gains an unexpected faculty, or a relationship becomes something the viewer can physically see. These choices can work in a completely different visual medium.

What we can infer

Mark’s 2005 email to Robin documents a deliberate attachment to pixelation: he drew in SuperPaint, painted in Photoshop, and valued a surface that some art directors considered obsolete. It establishes an intentional choice of medium. The method of developing metaphors below is our inference from his finished illustrations and their editorial contexts.

The collection contains 13 images. Nine belong to two Think:Act articles; two more have verified commission descriptions; the two original JPEGs have unresolved contexts. That gives us recurring devices and useful case studies, with limited coverage of his career. Treat the guide as a practical interpretation, not a transcript of his internal process.

The strongest working hypothesis is that the oddness serves a recognizable editorial relationship. An enlarged ear, a seeing hand, or a body made of interfaces remains understandable because the distortion is attached to an activity the article discusses.

Read the thought through the construction

Make an activity occupy the scene

Visible: an ear towers above a small figure; surrounding lines and arrows animate the space. It accompanies advice about candid communication, with listening discussed in the following text.

Reading: listening acquires the scale and presence normally given to a monument or protagonist. The image makes a faculty feel consequential without depicting a conventional meeting.

Directing move: identify the faculty the argument depends on, then change its scale or role. Let the rest of the scene respond to it. The reusable choice is that attention becomes a place or a presence; it does not require an ear in every new scene.

Let two kinds of knowing share a body

Visible: a hand carries eyes and facial features beside a section about intuition and experienced judgment.

Reading: the hand joins sensing to acting. The unexpected anatomy makes the abstract idea feel physical. The face also gives a conceptual diagram the presence of a character.

Directing move: give an object a faculty it needs to express the argument. A tool might listen, a container might remember, or a route might hesitate. Define what that faculty does before drawing its eyes or mouth. These proposed extensions are ours.

Make the system become anatomy

Visible: a screen with an eye, headphones, a glove-like hand, a ring, and bent limbs assemble into a moving figure. The commission concerns physical control of a virtual-reality game.

Reading: the interface and the user occupy one body. The pose connects the technical subject to a bodily experience.

Directing move: assign system functions to parts of the figure: receiving, seeing, acting, carrying, deciding. Their connections can show dependence or control. For another story, a character might be assembled from the objects of their work; which object gets to steer becomes the editorial question.

Give the space between people a job

Visible: two figures face one another; patterns and objects occupy their shared space. Their interiors use different arrangements of curves.

Reading: conversation becomes something spatial, crowded, and interpretable. The article’s discussion of differing meanings supports this reading; no single object has a verified translation.

Directing move: draw the relationship itself. What passes between the figures? What changes on arrival? What reaches one person and misses the other? Preserve enough ambiguity for discovery while making the central exchange readable.

Use a recurring character as a comparison

“Keep It Simple, Smarty-Pants” places related faces in a chart comparing kinds of simplicity. Repeated features make the changed head structures easy to compare. The chart labels anchor the categories; the shapes alone do not uniquely name them.

Directing move: keep an identity stable and change the part that carries the argument. This gives a series coherence and helps the viewer notice differences. Eyes need not mean surveillance; hands need not mean control; rays need not mean spirituality. Lines may be compositional rhythm or texture. Add a specific meaning only when the subject and the relationship support it.

A practical directing pass

  1. Name the claim and its tension. “Technology” is a subject. “The interface takes over the user’s senses” is a claim you can stage. Write what the viewer should understand, feel, or question.
  2. Find a bodily or spatial verb. Listen, hold, filter, translate, connect, balance, enter, carry. Translate the abstract sentence into something that can happen between forms.
  3. Choose one main transformation. Enlarge, substitute, fuse, contain, or vary. Give the transformed thing a specific role. Add a second device only if it clarifies the first.
  4. Stage a readable relationship. Establish a focal silhouette and a clear relation of scale, contact, direction, or position. Frontality, framing, and a platform can give an object the presence of an exhibit or performer.
  5. Set the emotional register. A useful extrapolation from the smiling hand, strange hero, and animated machine-body is a mixture of curiosity, humor, and unease. Choose the mixture the story needs; avoid applying it automatically to every subject.
  6. Test the image before decorating it. Describe the metaphor without mentioning the palette, texture, or artist. View it small. Remove one accessory at a time. Keep the details that improve meaning, hierarchy, or rhythm.

These steps are a proposed production method. We have not recovered Mark’s sketches or a statement showing this was his actual order of work.

Add the right amount

Accent: change a prop, a feature, or one scale relationship within the existing scene. Construction: rebuild the focal subject around a metaphor. Scene: let the setting participate in the same idea. Start with the smallest intervention that changes what the viewer understands.

For a new hypothetical brief, take the claim: “A planning tool should organize someone’s wishes without erasing their differences.”

  • Accent: a suitcase has compartments shaped to accept several unlike objects. The fit expresses accommodation; the distinct shapes remain visible.
  • Construction: give the organizer two contrasting hands. One stamps every object into the same rectangle; the other reshapes its compartments to receive the original forms. The hands perform competing kinds of simplification.
  • Scene: make an entire terminal conform to a standard rectangular opening, with a flexible route that lets the differently shaped travelers pass intact. The setting enlarges the same argument into an environment.

These are new concepts derived from the guide, not lost Ulrich designs. Choose one treatment rather than combining all three. Each could be drawn in graphite, built from paper, modeled in clay, or rendered in the project’s existing graphic language.

Let movement reveal the relationship

The sources here are still illustrations. The following motion directions are new extensions: a receiving organ grows as attention shifts; a signal changes form between speakers; a head structure rearranges while the face stays recognizable; a helping hand reveals what was supporting the apparently independent figure.

For each action, specify the initial relation, the change, and what the viewer understands afterward. Use motion to expose a dependence, transformation, or exchange. A loop of blinking eyes and pulsing rays can add activity, but its symbolic contribution still needs a reason.

Symbolic direction is independent of rendering. If the project also calls for Robin’s early Mac treatment, follow the separate rendering brief: fine raster edges, the specified fixed 256-color palette, and stable ordered dithering. That is a reconstruction target; it is not required for these metaphors to work.

Review the proposal

Ask the agent: “What is this image saying? Which physical relationship says it? Why is the strange feature useful? Which reference supports your choice? What can we remove?”

Keep the proposal when the metaphor survives a change of medium, the main relation reads at a glance, and the unusual details reward closer viewing. Revise it when the explanation depends on a stock collection of eyes, hands, machines, or bursts.
