# Achbar devices catalogue

Stable IDs: MC = *Manufacturing Consent: Noam Chomsky and the Media* (1992, Achbar and Wintonick).
TC = *The Corporation* (2003, Achbar and Abbott, written by Joel Bakan). Add new entries as MC15 and
TC15 onward; never renumber.

Each entry: what a source says happens, the source (IDs resolve to links at the bottom and in
`README.md`), and our reading. "Reading" is always INFERENCE, ours, not the filmmakers'. Quotes from
sources are under 15 words. No timestamps are given for *Manufacturing Consent*; no source we read had
any. Watch the scene before you build on it.

## Manufacturing Consent (1992)

### MC01 The newspaper on the operating table
- **What happens:** The filmmakers put on scrubs and play surgeons. With scalpel and sutures they cut a
  newspaper article about East Timor, while a heart monitor and respirator supply the sound [npr1993].
  Another reviewer calls it "surgeons filleting out column inches on a mock operating-table" [young2003].
  A third describes the filmmakers dressed as surgeons showing how the *Times* toned down a London story
  on a massacre [camus2009].
- **Source note:** None of these mention gloves. "Surgical-gloved hands" is a common retelling we could
  not confirm.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Editing is a procedure done by people with tools, so stage it as one. The
  joke is the solemn hospital sound under a cut that makes a story smaller. The props are cheap and
  literal; the frame does the arguing.

### MC02 Column inches unrolled in a stadium
- **What happens:** Two rolls of paper are laid on the floor of a darkened stadium and unrolled. One
  ends in seconds; the other keeps going while floodlights come on down the stadium. Then the screen
  gives the numbers: 1975-79, the *New York Times* ran 1175 column inches on Cambodia and 70 on East
  Timor [warne]. The 70 and 1175 figures are also in [levy]; the method of "stitching together all the
  columns of text" is in [numeroteca2013]. Wikipedia calls the East Timor study "a centerpiece of the
  film" [wiki-mc].
- **Related fact:** Wintonick told NPR they rented Montreal's Olympic stadium and paid the hydro company
  to turn on the lights [npr1993]. Whether that is this scene is our guess (INFERENCE), not stated.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The strongest device in either film. A ratio is turned into a physical length
  and revealed in real time, so the viewer feels the difference before reading the number. The number
  arrives last, as the verdict. The lights coming on are the clock.

### MC03 Chomsky on public screens
- **What happens:** The filmmakers played their Chomsky footage on a Sony Jumbotron in Times Square, in
  Montreal's Olympic stadium, and on "the largest point-of-purchase video wall," a 264-screen video cube
  in a shopping centre [npr1993]. Wintonick said they put him where people expect commercials, news or
  sports scores, to see how people respond; NPR reports the shoppers mostly ignored him [npr1993]. A
  Canadian review: "his image shows up on a giant video wall in a suburban mall" [bailey1993].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Put the dissenting idea into the machinery it criticises and let the setting
  comment. The passers-by who ignore it are part of the shot, not a failure of it.

### MC04 The stadium scoreboard and the coach
- **What happens:** Chomsky talks about the opiate-like effect of spectator sport while his speech is
  projected on a football scoreboard in an empty stadium, with Wintonick on the sideline as an
  unenthused coach [greene2013].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The setting is chosen to match the content (sport talk on a sports screen),
  and the filmmaker enters as a deadpan extra. The emptiness of the stadium is the punchline.

### MC05 The subject is always mediated
- **What happens:** "The professor is rarely presented 'clean'" [bailey1993]: on a mall video wall,
  reading a teleprompter, being interviewed, wired to microphones around the world. Bailey argues this
  stops us treating his words as gospel. Achbar told NPR the techniques remind viewers they are watching
  a media product [npr1993].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Never let the expert float on a neutral background. Show the screen, the mic,
  the monitor, the broadcast frame around them, so the viewer stays aware of the channel.

### MC06 Showing the strings
- **What happens:** The directors appear in the film uncredited; Wintonick's frame and haircut become
  familiar over Part 1 [young2003]. They leave shots long and leave the word "Cut" in when its delivery
  says something [camus2009]. In one scene Wintonick appears with his daughter [greene2013]. Zeitgeist
  says the film "encourages viewers to question its own workings" [zeitgeist-mc]. Greene says it resists
  hagiography through self-aware scenes "that foreground the film's construction" [greene2013].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The film admits it is made. That earns trust for a one-sided argument: the
  maker is visible, so the viewer can discount for him.

### MC07 Key phrases in block capitals
- **What happens:** Important statements are flashed on screen in capitals and held for several
  seconds [young2003]. Young calls this a gimmick.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** A text card used as an underline. Cheap, legible, and easy to overuse; the
  criticism is part of the lesson.

### MC08 The illustrative cut-away
- **What happens:** NPR's reviewer lists cuts from Chomsky to a Hitler rally, police arresting peace
  protesters, a church steeple beside a minaret [npr1993]. Young complains Chomsky rarely gets two
  sentences before a cut to "howlingly prosaic stock-footage or stills" [young2003]. Hicks found the
  images "never distracts from the message" [hicks1993].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Archive as illustration. It keeps a talk film moving, and it is the device
  critics most often call too literal. Use the cut when it adds a second idea, not a picture of the word.

### MC09 Let television interrupt itself
- **What happens:** In a heated TV debate with John Silber, as Chomsky makes a last attempt to finish,
  he is cut off by a quick mix to a national lottery ad [camus2009]. Erickson describes a broadcast that
  cuts to a commercial while Chomsky sits smiling [erickson2002]. Chomsky's argument about "concision"
  (time between commercials limits what can be said) is a major thread [bailey1993]; the companion book
  has a chapter "Concision: A Structural Constraint" (from a search extract, not read).
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Keep the broadcaster's own interruptions in the cut. The format makes the
  argument for you.

### MC10 Philosopher All-Stars trading cards
- **What happens:** The film has philosopher trading cards; NPR describes them as an imagined item from
  a future world [npr1993]. Wikipedia says the companion book reproduces eighteen "Philosopher All-Stars"
  cards "as seen in the film" (uncited there) [wiki-mc].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Borrow a pop-culture format (sports cards) for serious people. It flatters
  the audience's intelligence and makes thinkers collectable.

### MC11 A whole Times on a gymnasium floor
- **What happens:** Bailey's review mentions a sequence that "lays out an entire Times on a gymnasium
  floor" [bailey1993]. The sentence starts on a page our scan lacks, so its context is missing.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Probably the same family as MC02 (newsprint as floor area), possibly the same
  scene. Kept separate until someone watches it.

### MC12 Archive breadth, critics included
- **What happens:** About 185 archival sources [npr1993]; "the best of Noam Chomsky" from 120 hours over
  25 years [hinson1993]. Chomsky on campuses and small radio stations, opponents shouting over him
  [erickson2002]; Buckley "rolls his eyes" [peacemag1992]. Rebuttal voices appear (Wolfe, Buckley, a
  *Times* editor), though Levy and Young both say too briefly [levy, young2003].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** A portrait built from other people's broadcasts. The opponents' footage does
  double duty: it is the counter-argument and an example of the media behaviour under discussion.

### MC13 Two parts and an intermission
- **What happens:** Part 1 "Thought Control in a Democratic Society" (95 min) and Part 2 "Activating
  Dissent" (72 min) [hinson1993]. Achbar's company says the intermission was there "to enable engagement
  with activist tables in theatre lobbies" [ihpi]. Peace Magazine: "You need a breather" [peacemag1992].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Diagnosis first, then what to do. The structure is built for the room the
  film plays in, not only for the screen.

### MC14 Ending on alternative media
- **What happens:** Near the end there is a sequence on alternative media, grassroots publications and
  community broadcasting, which Bailey calls "central to both the film and its context" [bailey1993].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The film ends by pointing at tools the viewer can use. Same instinct as TC12.

## The Corporation (2003)

### TC01 The diagnostic checklist
- **What happens:** The corporation, a legal person, is assessed against a "Personality Diagnostic
  Checklist" built from actual WHO and DSM-IV criteria; four case studies (workers, human health,
  animals, the biosphere) tick the boxes until it "fully meets the diagnostic criteria" of a psychopath
  [presskit]. Robert Hare, an expert on psychopathy, was brought in "to legitimize it" [presskit]. The
  criteria listed include callous unconcern for others, deceitfulness, incapacity to experience guilt,
  reckless disregard for safety [studyguide]. Achbar: "Our brilliant graphic team at Bento Box designed a
  presentation"; when the first check mark came on, "there was a huge laugh" [presskit]. DVD chapter 6,
  "The Pathology of Commerce" [dvd-chapters].
- **Source note:** One blog says the checklist was Hare's PCL-R. The press kit names WHO and DSM-IV
  criteria; go with the press kit.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** A borrowed official form carries a radical claim. The criteria are real and
  sourced, an expert signs off, and the check marks land one at a time as punchlines in grim material.
  Achbar on the case studies: "It's pretty dark... but we made it work" [presskit].

### TC02 The "bad apple" opening
- **What happens:** Achbar recorded about 30 hours of off-air news during Enron and WorldCom, noticed
  everyone saying "bad apple", and the clips became the opening [presskit]. Rosenbaum calls it a
  "hilarious critique" of a sound-bite term [rosenbaum2004].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Find the phrase the media keep repeating, gather every instance, and make the
  repetition itself the first argument. The montage questions a word before the film offers its own.

### TC03 The metaphor Rorschach
- **What happens:** Every CEO and critic was asked for their metaphor of the corporation; "It became a
  Rorschach test" [presskit]. CEOs offered football teams, families, soaring eagles; critics offered
  Frankenstein and other monsters [presskit]. DVD chapter 1 lists eagle, whale, Frankenstein's monster
  [dvd-chapters]; Cheshire lists killer sharks and out-of-control monsters [cheshire2004].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Ask everyone the same image question, then cut the answers together. The
  disagreement between metaphors shows each speaker's values faster than any argument.

### TC04 Direct address through a half-silvered mirror
- **What happens:** "Inspired by Errol Morris' set-up but lower-tech", Achbar put a part-silvered mirror
  at 45 degrees in front of the lens, so subjects spoke into the camera while seeing his face [presskit].
  Shot on blue or green screen for flexibility in post, with a second camera at 90 degrees [presskit].
  The study guide prints the lighting plan: bluescreen, backlights, softlight, mirror [studyguide].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Interviewees look the viewer in the eye, and the background is a variable,
  not a location. That is what lets graphics and archive sit behind or beside a speaker.

### TC05 Chapter cards with clinical titles
- **What happens:** 23 chapters, from "What is a Corporation?" through "The Pathology of Commerce",
  "Monstrous Obligations", "Psycho Therapies" to "Prognosis" [dvd-chapters]. Kermode says the cards
  make the argument feel rambling [kermode2006]. Variety praised the "bold organizational scheme"
  (quoted in [wiki-tc]). The TV version ran as three hours: "Pathology of Commerce", "Planet Inc.",
  "Reckoning" [wiki-tc].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The titles keep the medical metaphor running from diagnosis to prognosis, so
  the frame of TC01 organises the whole film, not one sequence.

### TC06 Their own films against them
- **What happens:** "Footage from pop culture, advertising, TV news, and corporate propaganda" [readme2006].
  Rosenbaum notes "campy instructional film" footage from about fifty years earlier, used to support and
  to mock [rosenbaum2004]. Kermode found the archive interesting but the early cutting crude [kermode2006].
- **Source note:** Prelinger Archives is often assumed as the source of the industrial films; nothing we
  read confirms it.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The institution's own promotional voice, from a more confident decade, is
  the best witness against it. No narration is needed when the old film oversells.

### TC07 The pie fight for externalities
- **What happens:** In the "externalities" section (Milton Friedman: the effect of a deal on a third
  party), "the image associated with this is a pie fight" [studyguide; dvd-chapters ch. 4].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** A dry economic term gets a slapstick picture. The bystander hit by a pie is
  the third party.

### TC08 The flurry of TV news
- **What happens:** A flurry of TV news clips ridicules how corporate crime is framed [cheshire2004].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Same move as TC02: repetition across channels becomes the point.

### TC09 The film catches itself
- **What happens:** A "funny and clever interlude" on real-life product placement turns the film on
  itself [rosenbaum2004]. DVD chapter 15 "Triumph of the Shill": undercover marketing, staged encounters
  [dvd-chapters].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The self-reflexive habit of MC06 carried into the second film.

### TC10 Interviews first, pictures after
- **What happens:** Abbott ordered 800 pages of transcripts into a 33-hour assembly and would not touch
  B-roll until the interview structure worked [presskit]. Achbar: the narrative structure was locked
  first and images layered on after, which he calls a turning point [movieweb2004].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The graphics serve a spoken argument that already stands. A graphics pitch
  should name the line of interview it lands on.

### TC11 Insiders allowed to reveal themselves
- **What happens:** Verite footage of Pfizer VP Tom Klein, who "doesn't realize how much he is revealing"
  (editor's note in [presskit]). Achbar says media-trained interviews that stayed opaque were cut
  [movieweb2004]. Cheshire singles out a tea-serving scene with former Shell chairman Mark Moody-Stuart
  [cheshire2004] (who is served, and why, we have not confirmed).
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Give the powerful the floor and the time; the candid moment does more than
  the critic's line.

### TC12 A hopeful last act
- **What happens:** Final chapters "Psycho Therapies" and "Prognosis" show people fighting back
  [dvd-chapters]. Abbott: "We don't want people to emerge from this film feeling only despair"
  [presskit]. Achbar argued the ending must be hopeful [movieweb2004].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** The diagnosis comes with a treatment. Same instinct as MC13 and MC14.

### TC13 Ray Anderson's flight
- **What happens:** Interface CEO Ray Anderson compares industrial civilisation to an early flying
  machine launched off a cliff, its pilot feeling the wind and believing he flies while in free fall
  [cheshire2004, and search extracts of the transcript]. Reviewers call his story the film's most
  meaningful [rosenbaum2004].
- **Source note:** We have the spoken image. What is on screen during it (archive of failed flights or
  not) we have not confirmed.
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** A memorable spoken metaphor from an insider. If archive of failed early
  flights runs under it, that is TC06 meeting TC03.

### TC14 Sound
- **What happens:** Original music by Leonard J. Paul; sound design and music supervision by Velcrow
  Ripper; tracks by Yo La Tengo, Loscil, Do Make Say Think, Higher Intelligence Agency and Biosphere and
  others [presskit]. Narrator Mikela J. Mikael [presskit]. Kermode found the score intrusive
  [kermode2006]. The soundtrack was released free on the Internet Archive [wiki-tc].
- **Reading (INFERENCE):** Indie and ambient electronic textures rather than an orchestral score. Light
  narration; the interviews carry the voice.

## What critics praised and criticised

Praise: "imaginative visual playfulness" and wit [hinson1993]; "playful, frisky, wildly imaginative"
[greene2013]; "a remarkably light touch" for a heavy subject [rosenbaum2004]; "wit and visual spark"
[cheshire2004]; the Wall Street Journal's "fast-moving collage, an ironic surreal visual riff" and LA
Weekly's "Visually arresting and very funny" (both quoted in [presskit]).

Criticism: length (167 and 145 minutes) [levy, ebert2004]; graphics and staging as gimmicks that sit
awkwardly with grave material [young2003]; stock footage too literal [young2003]; graphics like "a
government information leaflet" and crude early cutting [kermode2006]; opponents given too little time
[levy, bailey1993]; the psychopath frame "seems like a lawyer's tactic" [rosenbaum2004].
