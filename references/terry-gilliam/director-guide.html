What is documented and what is inferred

Gilliam talks about method far more than meaning. His own account (the 1974 BBC lesson, G21) says the point is a story, a joke or an idea; that cut-outs are the quickest form he knows; that paper does sudden moves well and graceful ones badly; and that this is why his films lean to violence and sex. The method came first and the subjects grew into it.

Everything below that goes further, about why a found face on a cartoon body is funny or why the foot feels like authority, is our reading. PSYCHOLOGY.md sets out each reading with the framework it borrows (Bakhtin, Bergson, Freud, medieval marginalia) and labels it as inference. None of those writers is about Gilliam; the fit is our argument.

His animation is copyrighted, so this profile links to it and keeps no frames. The pictures on this page are public-domain plates of the kind he cut from. They are free for us to cut too.

Six moves, with anchors

1. Real face, silly body

Evidence. In 1974 he picks up an old photograph, admires the face, and gives it a cartoon body. In Holy Grail God is an animated photograph of the cricketer W. G. Grace (G23).

Reading. The face keeps its dignity; only the body is made ridiculous. Authority is uncrowned without being destroyed, which is why the joke reads as affectionate rather than cruel.

Apply it. Find an anonymous public-domain sitter who reads as "patriarch", "official" or "respectable lady". Keep the face still and serious. Give it a body that wobbles, rolls or comes apart. For a boss in one of Bob's songs, a bearded Victorian manager on a typewriter body that clatters when he talks. That variation is ours.

2. Make the transition the joke

Evidence. His job on Flying Circus was the links. The Pythons wanted a stream of consciousness and his animation carried one sketch into the next (G05). Terry Jones hired that style from his children's-show work (G04).

Reading. A link moves by resemblance, the way a dream does. The viewer enjoys seeing a shape in one scene become a different thing in the next.

Apply it. End each scene on one element and let it become the first element of the next: same place on screen, roughly the same shape, a new meaning. A skipping rope becomes a telephone cord becomes a noose of red tape. Chain three or four. This is also Robin's standing rule that scene changes follow an element instead of fading.

3. Let the thing eat its user

Evidence. In Killer Cars (G10) cars eat pedestrians, a giant mutant cat eats a building, and a giant hand kills the cat. A television hurts its viewer's eyes (G17).

Reading. The convenience consumes the consumer. Then the cure is worse than the disease, and a larger force ends it.

Apply it. Give the product, the platform or the institution a mouth. Escalate once with a "solution" that is worse, then stop it from outside. The target must be a system, never a person.

4. Stop it from above

Evidence. The foot is cut from Cupid's foot in Bronzino's allegory (G06). Gilliam recalled that it looked about to crush the dove, and that he saw it as punctuation, a sudden halt.

Reading. The ending that refuses to explain itself. It is funny because it solves every sketch-writer's problem honestly, and a little frightening because power arrives without a reason.

Apply it. Once per film, at a section that has run its course. Our own object, never the foot: a rubber stamp, a gavel, a flat iron, a thumb cut from an engraving.

5. Work in the margin

Evidence. He reportedly said the Holy Grail animations drew on a book of medieval manuscript margins (G25), and he names Bosch and Bruegel as favourites.

Reading. The scribes let rabbits fight knights and foxes preach in the margins of sacred books. Gilliam's animation sat in the margins of the sketches and had the same licence.

Apply it. When a film has a main text (Bob singing, a lyric on screen) give the edges a second voice that comments on it: small figures in the corners doing what the main picture will not.

6. Lie about scale, and say the bad news calmly

Evidence. In 1974 he shows perfume bottles becoming a city and rocks becoming mountains (G21). In The Prince and the Black Spot a level voice reports a death from an ignored spot (G14).

Reading. The camera cannot tell how big anything is, and the voice cannot tell how bad anything is. Both gaps are jokes the audience is in on.

Apply it. Build a world quickly from small found things shown huge. Put a calm narrator or caption over the panic. Keep the deadpan for songs that already look at trouble with dark humour.

What our own test taught us

We have tried this once: bobwiseman-wgt-gilliam, a Gilliam-manner pass on We Got Time chapter 8, the boss (206-245 s), on 2026-10-04. It paints procedural steel engravings, hand-tints them, lays them on airbrushed skies and moves them on single pivots on twos and threes. That part matches his account.

Two things differ from his 1974 method. The test cuts each piece with a pale paper margin and gives it a soft drop shadow. Gilliam blacked every cut edge with a felt-tip and fought shadows with high lights and a glass plate. The next Gilliam pass should try a dark rim and flat light, or choose the paper-theatre edge on purpose and say so.

From the Midjourney runs: putting "Gilliam" in a prompt pulls Python collage pastiche. Ask the generator for the source material (an 1880s cabinet portrait, a steel engraving of a bicycle) and build the collage ourselves.

Where it fits Bob's songs

These are proposals, not decisions. Bob's films use allegory and no real politicians.

  • Libelous. The unnamed company as a hungry giant built from catalogue engravings, eating its own customers, stopped by a falling rubber stamp. Moves 3 and 4.
  • The Reform Party at Burning Man. The party's effigy as a Victorian strongman photograph on a wicker body that swallows its voters, then burns. Moves 1 and 3.
  • We Got Time, the boss. Keep the existing test and add a chain of visual rhymes through the office: in-tray, conveyor, filing drawer, mouth. Move 2.

Rules that keep it honest

  • Punch up. The targets are power, pomposity, institutions and products.
  • Keep the pain paper. Visible cut edges and comic sounds; no realistic injury.
  • No recognisable real people. Old photographs of anonymous sitters give the Victorian authority without naming anyone.
  • No Python trademarks: not the foot, not the Liberty Bell, not the castaway's "It's".
  • The test: can you explain the joke without saying "Gilliam" or "Monty Python"? If it only works as a pastiche, start again.
