30 pieces of Gilliam's animation (G01-G30) and 8 public-domain source plates (S01-S08). Gilliam's cut-out animation from 1968 to 1983: the pre-Python children's and variety shows, Monty Python's Flying Circus, the 1974 BBC demonstration, and the animation in the Python films. Not a complete list; Flying Circus has many unnamed links that no source itemises. His live-action features are covered in biography.json, not here.

Gilliam's animation is copyrighted (Python (Monty) Pictures, the BBC and others). This catalogue cites and links it and holds no copies. The S plates are public-domain images of the kind he is documented cutting from; they are committed in plates/ with provenance in sources/plates-manifest.json.

'What happens' comes only from the cited sources. 'Reading' is our inference and is labelled so. Where a source gives only a title, the entry says so; watch the piece before treating it as a visual anchor.

Start with: G21, the 1974 lesson, G06, the foot, G10, Killer Cars, G23, God as a photograph, then the director's guide.

G02 · 1968 · sourced

The Christmas Card

Do Not Adjust Your Set (Rediffusion), Christmas segment

What happens (sourced). Victorian Christmas cards come to life and misbehave. Gilliam found the images in the Tate's collection of Victorian Christmas cards, photocopied them and rearranged them. He later said the style came out of that process, not out of a plan: he took the quickest, easiest route and used pictures he loved.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the first piece already has the whole method. A polite, sentimental, respectable picture is cut free and made to do what it was printed to prevent.

Use. The model for a season or holiday piece: start from the sweetest found image and let it go wrong. Plate S08 is a Raphael Tuck card of this type. Plates: S08

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qXW9b9O9S6A. Third-party upload (The Monty Python Museum) of Storytime, which includes The Christmas Card.

G03 · 1968 · sourced

Storytime

Short compilation of his early cut-out pieces

What happens (sourced). Opens like a children's reading, then follows a cockroach running wild in a house, a strange tale about Albert Einstein, and holiday cards that come to life. It already has a stomping foot, later used as a Python gag.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the storybook frame is the respectable container and the cockroach is what crawls out of it. The foot is there before Python, as a way to end things.

Use. A frame story (a reading, a lesson, a broadcast) that loses control of its own contents.

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qXW9b9O9S6A. Third-party upload; its description lists Don the Cockroach, the Hands and The Christmas Card.

G05 · 1969-1974 · sourced in part; per-series detail not in our sources

Flying Circus opening titles

Monty Python's Flying Circus (BBC), every series

What happens (sourced). Built around Sousa's Liberty Bell march, which Gilliam chose because it was in the public domain. The sequence changed each series. In the 2019 restoration featurette Gilliam and the restorers watch 'this chap's head opening up' in the series 1 titles. The foot (G06) comes down at the end and crushes the title.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the titles teach the audience the rules in thirty seconds. Respectable things will open, change into other things, and be stopped from above.

Use. Anchor for the opening head and for chained transitions. Do not reuse the march or the foot.

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QSoNNtlQqDs. Official Monty Python channel, 2019: Gilliam watches the restored series 1 titles and animation.

G06 · 1969 onward · sourced

The Foot

Flying Circus titles and links; the Python emblem

What happens (sourced). A giant foot descends and squashes whatever is on screen, including the show's title at the end of every series' opening. It is cut from Cupid's foot in Bronzino's Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time (National Gallery, London), usually flipped and rotated. In a 2024 documentary for the gallery's 200th year Gilliam recalled that the foot looked about to crush the dove beside it, and that he saw it as a punctuation mark, a sudden halt to whatever was going on.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the ending that refuses to explain itself. See PSYCHOLOGY.md, The Foot.

Use. A hard stop, once per film, with our own descending object. Never the foot itself. Plates: S01 S02

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QSoNNtlQqDs. Seen at the end of the titles in the official restoration featurette.

G10 · 1970 · sourced; episode and narrator disputed

Killer Cars

Flying Circus episode 22, 'How to Recognise Different Parts of the Body' (series 2, 24 November 1970). Cinema Cats places it in a different episode.

What happens (sourced). Cars hunt and eat pedestrians to cut down their numbers. A giant mutant cat towers over London, scares the cars off and eats a building, then a giant hand kills it. One review quotes narration blaming atomic mutation; another source has the narrator joking about the budget. The official biography singles out the giant cat prowling London.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the thing you consume consumes you, the cure is worse than the disease, and the cure is crushed from above. Escalation, then a stop.

Use. Anchor for 'let the thing eat its user' and for escalation that ends with a larger force.

G14 · 1969 (heard in the series 1 restoration featurette) · narration sourced; visuals not described

The Prince and the Black Spot

Flying Circus, series 1 (episode not identified in our sources)

What happens (sourced). A level narrator reports that a prince discovered a spot on his face, foolishly ignored it, and three years later died of cancer. A weak source says the BBC later redubbed 'cancer' as 'gangrene' on some releases; unconfirmed.

Reading (inference). Our reading: a fairy-tale frame meets a medical death, told flat. The gap between voice and content is the joke. See PSYCHOLOGY.md, part 1 section 6.

Use. Anchor for deadpan over disaster. The most painful register in the catalogue; never aim it at a real person's illness.

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QSoNNtlQqDs. Official restoration featurette; the narration is heard near the end.

G21 · 1974 · sourced from the transcript

The Do-It-Yourself Film Animation Show: Terry Gilliam on cut-out animation

BBC, presented by Bob Godfrey

What happens (sourced). Gilliam's own fifteen-minute lesson. Animation is for telling a story, making a joke or expressing an idea; technique comes second; cut-outs are the quickest form he knows. He gives a found photograph's face a cartoon body, builds an old man with two legs and a bending lady in three hinged parts, does a fight inside a pram with bits of black paper, blackens every cut edge, fights shadows with high lights and a glass plate, and fakes scale (perfume bottles become a city, rocks become mountains). He says sudden moves are what paper does best, which is why his films lean to violence and sex, and tells the viewer to cheat: wheels instead of walking legs, a dropping jaw instead of mouth shapes.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the method chose the subjects. See PSYCHOLOGY.md, The first fact.

Use. The primary source for STYLE-BRIEF.txt. Watch this before anything else.

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LaG_EiFX7a0. BBC Archive, official clip (2022 upload).

G22 · 1974 (one upload says 1975) · sourced

The Miracle of Flight

Standalone short

What happens (sourced). A history of human flight. People leap off cliffs flapping wings and fail, so they try birds. A king in 1643 pushes men off a tower. Aeroplanes still do not get it right.

Reading (inference). Our reading: aspiration meets gravity, and the fall is funny. The theme returns as Sam Lowry's flying dreams in Brazil (1985).

Use. Anchor for longing and escape lyrics: a glorious attempt, a comic fall.

Watch. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1BKEEJfUVlE. Third-party upload (FilmZ), titled 'Miracle of Flight (1975)'.

G25 · 1975; restored 2015 · sourced at second hand

Holy Grail animations and the cut footage

Monty Python and the Holy Grail; 40th-anniversary Blu-ray

What happens (sourced). On the 40th-anniversary disc Gilliam reportedly said the film's animations drew on a book about the illustrations in the margins of medieval manuscripts; the title he gave has not been traced. About 14 minutes of animation cut from the 1975 release were found and added to that disc, introduced by Gilliam. Separately, he and Terry Jones name Bruegel and Bosch as favourites.

Reading (inference). Our reading: his place in the show was the margin, where the doodler is licensed to say what the main text cannot.

Use. Anchor for a side channel of commentary around a main performance. Plates S05, S06 and S07 are the free material. Plates: S05 S06 S07

G28 · recurring, 1969-1974 · sourced in general terms; no single piece named

Venus, Rodin and the painted nudes

Flying Circus collage material

What happens (sourced). The 2010 CNN interview notes that his animations took famous works such as Botticelli's Venus and Rodin's sculptures and put them in absurd situations. In 1974 he said cut-outs tend toward the violent or the sexy.

Reading (inference). Our reading: the classical nude is nudity made respectable, and making Venus blush or wobble shows what the respectability covers.

Use. For Bob's films keep it at a painted Venus being shy, never leering. Plate S04 is Botticelli's Birth of Venus. Plates: S04 S01
