# Terry Gilliam: the psychology of the symbols

Written 2026-10-15 for the reference library. This is the deep file. `AGENT-DIRECTION.txt` is the one-page working
tool; this explains why its moves work on a viewer.

## Evidence labels

Every paragraph says what kind of claim it makes.

- **[G]** Gilliam's own account, with a source id from `sources.json`.
- **[S]** A secondary source (critic, journalist, encyclopedia).
- **[F]** A framework we bring from outside: a named book or theory. Gilliam never cites these for his cartoons.
- **[I]** Our inference. Useful for directing, not a fact about his mind.

Gilliam talks about method far more than meaning. When he does talk about meaning, he usually plays it down. So
most of the psychology below is [F] and [I], and it should be held loosely.

## The first fact: the meaning grows out of the paper

On the 1974 BBC show [G, bbc1974], Gilliam lays out his whole philosophy in a few minutes. Animation is for telling a
story, making a joke, or expressing an idea; technique comes second; cut-outs are the quickest form he knows. Then he
says something that explains more of his imagery than any theory does. Paper pieces cannot move smoothly. Quick,
sudden moves are easy and funny. So his films lean toward violence and sex, because those are the subjects that
sudden moves serve. He adds that he is always being accused of violence, and that this is the reason.

Read that as a psychological fact about the work [I]. The dismemberments, the crushing foot, the heads popping
open are partly a style chosen by a constraint. The constraint came first and the meaning grew into it. A director
using this profile should copy that order: find what the medium does easily and badly, then let the jokes and the
symbols come from what it does easily.

Two more remarks from the same show support this [G, bbc1974]. He tells the viewer to cheat as much as possible:
put the man on wheels so his legs never move, give a talker a cut-out jaw that drops instead of drawn mouth shapes.
And he says the film comes alive under the camera, where playing with the pieces produces surprises you keep.
His storyboards are crude reminders, not plans.

## Part 1. The engine: why the pictures feel the way they do

### 1. Found authority, misbehaving

What he did: cut faces and bodies out of Victorian photographs, Christmas cards, Renaissance paintings and family
albums and made them do undignified things [G, bbc1974; farout2021; expansion2010]. On the 1974 show he picks up a
photograph and says the man's face is tremendous, so give it a cartoon body.

Framework [F, bakhtin1965]: Mikhail Bakhtin's *Rabelais and His World* describes carnival laughter as
"degradation": dragging the high, the official and the spiritual down to the body, to eating, sex, birth and death.
Carnival crowns a fool and uncrowns a king. The point is renewal, not hatred.

Our reading [I]: a Victorian portrait photograph carries two kinds of authority at once. It is a document (this
person existed and sat still for the camera) and it is a performance of respectability (the stiff collar, the
moustache, the posed hand). Giving that face a rubbery cartoon body uncrowns it without destroying it. The face stays
dignified and real; only the body is made ridiculous. That split, a serious real face on a silly drawn body, is the
basic Gilliam joke, and it is a carnival uncrowning in miniature.

Why Victorian [I, built on S and G]: Gilliam was an American who moved to London in 1967 [S, wikipedia]. To an
outsider, the Victorian past that British institutions still dressed in looked like a costume. The material was also
cheap or free, which he says was the actual reason he used it [G, expansion2010]. Both readings can be true. The
outsider's eye sees the costume; the budget hands him the scissors.

### 2. The machine in the man

Framework [F, bergson1900]: Henri Bergson's *Laughter* (1900) argues that we laugh when a living person behaves
like a mechanism: rigid, repetitive, jerked about by forces they do not notice. His formula is something mechanical
encrusted on the living.

Our reading [I]: a cut-out figure is Bergson's theory made literal. The body is a set of hinged parts. It moves in
fixed increments (Gilliam moved pieces by marks on a template, one step per frame [G, bbc1974]). Heads lift off like
lids because the head really is a separate piece of paper. A Victorian gentleman who already looked starched in his
photograph becomes a puppet, and the audience laughs at the stiffness it always suspected was there.

The psychology underneath: the fear that we are less alive than we pretend, that manners and institutions have
turned people into mechanisms. Gilliam's later feature films make that fear explicit in office machinery and ducts
(*Brazil*, 1985). The animation already has it, as a joke.

### 3. Licensed aggression

Framework [F, freud1905]: in *Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious* (1905), Freud splits jokes into innocent
ones and "tendentious" ones, and says tendentious jokes come in two kinds: hostile and obscene. They let us enjoy
aggression or sexuality that would otherwise be blocked, because the joke form disarms the inner censor.

Our reading [I]: Gilliam's own summary of what cut-outs do well, violence and sex [G, bbc1974], matches Freud's two
kinds of tendentious joke almost exactly. The paper medium is the disarming device. Nobody is hurt when a paper man is
squashed; the cut edge is visible proof that it is only paper. That is why the violence reads as innocent to many
viewers [S, expansion2010; flavorwire], and why Gilliam could say in 2010 that the style was not violent [G,
expansion2010] while saying in 1974 that he is always accused of violence [G, bbc1974]. Both are true. The aggression
is real; the pain is not depicted.

Directing consequence: the aggression has to stay visibly paper. The moment a cut-out bleeds realistically or a
victim's suffering is shown, the license is revoked and the joke turns cruel.

### 4. Dream-work in the links

What the show used him for: his animations linked sketches, often merging with the live action, because the
Pythons wanted the show to feel like a stream of consciousness [S, wikipedia-mpfc]. His stream-of-consciousness style
in the children's show *Do Not Adjust Your Set* is what impressed Terry Jones [S, flavorwire].

Framework [F, freud1900]: in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900), Freud describes the "dream-work": condensation
(several ideas fused into one image) and displacement (energy sliding from one object to a neighbour that looks or
sounds like it). Dreams move by resemblance, not logic. The Surrealists built a practice on the same idea, and a
critic compared Gilliam's animation to Max Ernst's collages before Gilliam had heard of Ernst [G, dazed2015].

Our reading [I]: a Gilliam link is a dream sequence with a deadline. A shape in the outgoing sketch becomes a shape
in the next thing because they rhyme visually, and the audience accepts the move because dreams have trained us to.
The pleasure is recognition: "oh, that was always going to turn into that." This is the same thing Robin asked for
in *In Her Dream* and *Clap Hands*: scene changes that follow an element as it shrinks, grows or morphs into the next
scene (memory `transitions-follow-an-element`). Gilliam is the canonical teacher of it.

### 5. The uncanny of a photo on a cartoon

Framework [F, freud1919]: Freud's essay "The Uncanny" (1919), building on Ernst Jentsch, ties uncanniness to doubt
about whether something is alive (dolls, automata) and to detached body parts (a severed head, a hand cut off at the
wrist, feet that dance by themselves).

Our reading [I]: Gilliam's figures sit on that line on purpose. A photographed face is the most "alive" kind of image
there is, and it is attached to a body that is obviously not alive. Detached limbs are everywhere because the pieces
literally detach. What saves it from horror is speed and sound: a pop, a squeak, a raspberry, and the moment is over.
Slow it down or remove the comic sound and the same image becomes a nightmare. That is a useful dial, and Gilliam's
later films turn it toward the nightmare end.

### 6. The calm voice over catastrophe

What happens: in *The Prince and the Black Spot*, a level narrator reports that a prince noticed a spot on his face,
foolishly ignored it, and died of cancer three years later [heard in the 2019 restoration featurette, mpfc2019]. The
BBC later redubbed "cancer" as "gangrene" on some versions [S, weak: watchmojo].

Framework [F]: comedy theory calls this incongruity, a gap between register and content. Psychoanalysis would call
the narrator's tone "isolation of affect": stating something terrible with the feeling removed, which is one of the
ways people cope with fear of illness and death.

Our reading [I]: the deadpan voice performs British composure while the picture performs panic. The audience
laughs at the gap, and part of the laugh is relief that someone else said the frightening word calmly. Gilliam's 1978
book is called *Animations of Mortality* [S, encyclopedia]. Mortality is not a side theme. It is the punchline he
keeps returning to.

## Part 2. Symbol lexicon

Each entry: where it appears (catalogue IDs in `artwork-index.json`), what Gilliam said, the frameworks it touches,
our reading, and how to use it. Symbols in his work have no fixed dictionary meaning. Read each one in its scene.

### The Foot (G05, G06; source plates S01, S02)

- **Where:** the opening titles of every series, where it crushes the show's title [S, wikipedia-mpfc], and as a
  recurring stop. Cut from Cupid's foot in Bronzino's *Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time* (c. 1545, National Gallery,
  London) [S, wikipedia-mpfc; artnet2024].
- **What he said:** in a 2024 documentary for the National Gallery's 200th year, he recalled that Cupid's foot looked
  about to crush the dove beside it, and that it would make a lovely punctuation, a sudden halt to whatever was going
  on [G, via artnet2024]. Look at S02: the foot hangs over the dove exactly as he describes.
- **Frameworks:** [F] the deus ex machina, inverted. In classical theatre a god descends to save the plot; here a
  body part descends to end it. [F] Freud's super-ego, the parental voice that arrives from above and stops the fun.
  [F] Bakhtin's degradation: a detail from a Mannerist allegory of love and deceit becomes a slapstick weapon.
- **Our reading [I]:** the foot is the ending that refuses to explain itself. Power does not justify its arrival. It
  is funny because every sketch-writer knows the problem of how to end a sketch, and the foot is the most honest
  answer: stop it. It is also a little frightening for the same reason.
- **Use it when:** a section has run its course and needs a hard stop, or a pompous thing needs flattening from
  outside the frame. Once per film is plenty.
- **Don't:** copy the foot itself. It is Gilliam's trademark and the Pythons' logo. Find our own descending object
  (a rubber stamp, a gavel, a flat iron, a giant thumb from a PD engraving) that does the same job.

### The opening head (G05)

- **Where:** the title sequences, where a man's head opens up (Gilliam and the restorers discuss "this chap's head
  opening up" in the 2019 featurette [mpfc2019]).
- **What he said:** nothing about meaning. Technically, a head made of separate paper layers can be hinged open
  cheaply (his demo lady's head is about five layers of paper deep [G, bbc1974]).
- **Frameworks:** [F] Bergson's mechanism; [F] Freud's topographic mind (a container with depths); the old image of
  the head as a cabinet.
- **Our reading [I]:** the inner life shown as furniture. Thoughts are objects that can be taken out and looked at.
  It is intrusive (privacy pried open) and liberating (everything inside comes out to play) at the same time.
- **Use it when:** a character's private thought, memory or obsession needs to become visible, especially a
  respectable character's.

### Devouring things (G10, G17)

- **Where:** *Killer Cars* (cars eat pedestrians; a giant mutant cat towers over London, eats a building, and is
  killed by a giant hand) [S, flavorwire; cinemacats; montypython-bio]; *TV is Bad for Your Eyes* [S, wikipedia-mpfc
  episode list; montypython-bio]; the 1974 pram with a fight going on inside it [G, bbc1974].
- **Frameworks:** [F] Bakhtin's grotesque body is all mouths and openings; it swallows the world and is swallowed.
  [F] The postwar monster film, where atomic mutation makes a familiar animal enormous (one review quotes the
  *Killer Cars* narration on atomic mutation [S, cinemacats]).
- **Our reading [I]:** the conveniences of modern life (the car, the television, the pram, the toothpaste advert)
  turn on their users and eat them. It is consumer satire with the logic of a nightmare: the thing you consume
  consumes you. Then the cure is worse than the disease (a giant cat), and the cure is crushed by an even larger hand.
  Escalation, then a stop.
- **Use it when:** a song is about a system, a product or an institution that uses people up. It fits *Libelous*
  (the unnamed company as a hungry giant) and *The Reform Party at Burning Man* (the party's effigy swallowing its
  voters) without naming anyone.

### The betraying body (G14, G15)

- **Where:** *The Prince and the Black Spot* [mpfc2019]; *Investigating the Body* (S3 E27) and the episode titled
  *How to Recognise Different Parts of the Body* (S2 E22) [S, wikipedia-mpfc].
- **Frameworks:** [F] the uncanny of the body as a stranger; [F] isolation of affect in the narration; the medical
  diagram as a stage.
- **Our reading [I]:** the body is a machine you do not control, and it can kill you over a spot you ignored. The
  fear of illness gets a cartoon body so it can be looked at. Gilliam's choice of book title, *Animations of
  Mortality*, says this is central.
- **Use it carefully:** this is the most painful register. In a music video it suits a song that already looks at
  death with dark humour. Never aim it at a real person's illness.

### The lie about scale (G10, G21)

- **Where:** the giant cat over London [S, montypython-bio]; and his explicit lesson in 1974: perfume bottles out of
  context, with people placed at the right size, become a futuristic city; rocks against a sky with a little tone
  become mountains [G, bbc1974].
- **Frameworks:** [F] the child's-eye world, where adults and objects are giant; [F] the monster film again.
- **Our reading [I]:** the camera cannot tell you how big anything is. Gilliam enjoys that as a trick and as a kind
  of power: whoever controls the scale controls the drama. A small object made huge becomes a god or a monster; a
  huge institution made tiny becomes a toy.
- **Use it when:** you need a world fast and cheaply. Build cities from bottles, mountains from pebbles, and let the
  audience enjoy noticing.

### God as a found photograph (G23; source plate S03)

- **Where:** in *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* (1975), God is an animated photograph of the cricketer W. G. Grace,
  voiced by Graham Chapman [S, wikipedia-grail].
- **Frameworks:** [F] Freud's *The Future of an Illusion* (1927) reads the idea of God as a projection of the
  childhood father: bearded, powerful, cross.
- **Our reading [I]:** the highest authority in the film is a bearded Victorian sportsman pasted into the clouds. It
  deflates the sacred, but affectionately: Grace's grumpy face is exactly what a child imagines God looks like. The
  joke is that the projection is visible.
- **Use it when:** a voice from above (a boss, a court, a party leader) needs to appear. Use an anonymous PD face
  that reads as "patriarch", not a recognisable public figure.

### The animator who dies (G24)

- **Where:** in *Holy Grail*, the Legendary Black Beast of Aaaaarrrrrrggghhh chases the knights until its animator
  (played by Gilliam) has a fatal heart attack; the beast vanishes [S, wikipedia-grail].
- **Frameworks:** [F] metafiction: the work admits it is made.
- **Our reading [I]:** the monster exists only while a fragile person keeps drawing it. The world ends when its maker
  does. It is a joke about deadlines and about mortality, and it ties back to *Animations of Mortality*.
- **Use it when:** a film wants a moment of self-awareness, such as the hand of the animator entering the frame.
  Little Bob already plays a similar role in Bob's films.

### Flight and the fall (G22)

- **Where:** *The Miracle of Flight* (1974; one upload says 1975): people leap off cliffs flapping wings, try birds,
  and a 1643 king pushes men off a tower [S, flavorwire].
- **Frameworks:** [F] Icarus.
- **Our reading [I]:** aspiration meets gravity, and authority sends other people to test the dream. The theme grows
  into the flying dreams of Sam Lowry in *Brazil* (1985), set against a bureaucracy that cannot dream at all.
- **Use it when:** a lyric is about longing or escape. Let the attempt be glorious and the fall be funny, not tragic.

### The margins of sacred books (G25; source plate S07)

- **Where:** Gilliam has said the *Holy Grail* animations drew on a book about the illustrations in the margins of
  medieval manuscripts [G, reported by dangerousminds-grail; the book title he gave has not been traced]. He and
  Terry Jones name Bruegel and Bosch as favourites and find the medieval view of the world more vivid than modern
  imagery [G, expansion2010].
- **Frameworks:** [F] medieval marginalia (the "drolleries") put rude, violent and absurd scenes in the margins of
  psalters and books of hours: rabbits fighting knights, foxes preaching to geese (S07). They are licensed
  irreverence, tolerated at the edge of the sacred text.
- **Our reading [I]:** Gilliam's job on *Flying Circus* was marginalia. His animations were drawn in the gaps between
  the main text (the sketches), and that position gave him a license the sketches did not have. The margin is where
  the doodler can say what the centre cannot.
- **Use it when:** a film has a "main text" (a performance, a lyric on screen) and needs a side channel of commentary.

### High art as raw material (G20; source plates S01, S04)

- **Where:** Botticelli's Venus and Rodin's sculptures put in absurd situations [S, expansion2010]; *The Statue*,
  built from cut-outs of Michelangelo's *David* [S, flavorwire]. When he ran out of ideas he walked the National
  Gallery and came out with a flood of them [G, expansion2010]. The *Flying Circus* theme, Sousa's *Liberty Bell*, was
  chosen because it was in the public domain [S, wikipedia-mpfc].
- **Frameworks:** [F] Bakhtin again (the sacred brought down to the market square); [F] Dada's attack on art's
  solemnity.
- **Our reading [I]:** nothing is too grand to cut up. A masterpiece is a pile of parts waiting for a job. The free
  material is also the respectable material, which makes the defacement cheaper and funnier at once.
- **Use it when:** the brief wants grandeur punctured. Public-domain paintings are free, legal and loaded with
  cultural authority, which is exactly the charge the joke needs.

### Respectable sex (G19, G28)

- **What he said:** cut-outs tend to be violent or sexy [G, bbc1974]. The *Fliegender Zirkus* specials list a
  *Flashers' Love Story* [S, wikipedia-mpfc]. Nudes from paintings recur in his collage material.
- **Frameworks:** [F] Freud's obscene joke, the second kind of tendentious joke.
- **Our reading [I]:** classical nudes are the respectable form of nudity, and making Venus blush or wobble exposes
  the prurience under the respectability.
- **Use with care:** choose the register per artist and audience. For Bob's films, keep it at the level of a
  painted Venus being shy, never leering.

## Part 3. The person behind the pictures

Sourced facts first, readings after. Full biography with sources is in `biography.json`.

- Born 22 November 1940 in Minneapolis; family moved to Panorama City, Los Angeles, in 1952 [S, wikipedia].
- As a teenager he read *Mad*, edited by Harvey Kurtzman; he later said *Mad* inspired everything the Pythons did
  [S, wikipedia]. In 2015 he named *Mad* the magazine of his youth and Kurtzman's team brilliant [G, dazed2015].
- BA in political science, Occidental College, 1962 [S, wikipedia]. Worked on Kurtzman's *Help!*, where a photo
  strip featured John Cleese [S, wikipedia; montypython-bio].
- The official Python biography says drawing cartoons shaped his school life and his sceptical attitude toward
  authority [S, montypython-bio]. One biography says he left the US in the late 1960s partly because he feared being
  drawn into militant politics, after being stopped by police who assumed he used drugs [S, weak: imdb-bio; not
  checked against his own account].
- Moved to England in 1967; British citizen 1968; renounced US citizenship in 2006 [S, wikipedia].
- Loved Disney's craft and the Grimm stories under its films; loves the Surrealists because they make the mind work
  to make sense of them; first heard of Max Ernst when a critic compared his animation to Ernst [G, dazed2015].
- Calls Bosch and Bruegel favourites [G, expansion2010].

Readings [I]:

- **The outsider-satirist.** *Mad* taught him to parody the forms of media (ads, comics, TV). In Britain he applied
  that to the visual furniture of British respectability. The outsider sees the costume.
- **The sceptic of authority.** Kings, gods, bosses, policemen and officials are the main targets in his animation and
  his films. The foot is authority with no face at all.
- **The pragmatic trickster.** He presents himself as lazy and uses whatever works [G, bbc1974]. The trickster
  archetype (Hermes, Coyote) wins by shortcut and theft. His whole method is theft from the respectable, used fast.
- **The dreamer against the system.** The later films (*Time Bandits* 1981, *Brazil* 1985, *Munchausen* 1988, *12
  Monkeys* 1995) keep setting imagination against bureaucracy, asylums and official reality. The animation is where
  that argument started, played for laughs.

## Part 4. Using it responsibly in our films

- **Punch up.** The targets are power, pomposity, institutions and products. A cut-out joke at the expense of the
  weak is just bullying with scissors.
- **Keep the pain paper.** Visible paper edges and comic sounds keep the license. No realistic injury.
- **No real public figures.** Gilliam used W. G. Grace. We do not use recognisable real people, and for Bob's films
  the rule is allegory with no politicians (memory `bob-reform-party`). Old public-domain photographs of anonymous
  sitters give the Victorian authority without naming anyone.
- **Do not decode universally.** The foot is first a punctuation mark. Eyes, hands and mouths mean what their scene
  makes them mean.
- **One big stop per film.** The arbitrary ending is strong because it is rare.

## Part 5. Three artists, three questions

| Reference | Directing question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mark Von Ulrich | Which bodily or spatial change makes the argument visible? | An enormous ear makes listening dominate the scene. |
| Joel Nakamura | What story can this object inhabit, and what lives inside it? | An ark becomes a DNA lab. |
| Terry Gilliam | What respectable thing can we cut out of its world, make misbehave, and turn into the next thing? | A cricketer's photograph becomes God; Cupid's foot ends the sketch. |

They combine well. Mark gives the idea a body; Joel gives the body a world inside; Terry cuts the body out of an
old book, makes it misbehave, and drops a foot on it.
